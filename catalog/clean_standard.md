# Marracat Clean — the badge standard

The blue shield means the brand publishes an ingredient list and that list
clears the rules below. We check the list the brand publishes; we can't
confirm it is complete (owner, 2026-10-24). It is computed by `catalog/clean_badge.py` at
build time from the brand's own product page. It is a formulation check, not a
safety certification and not medical advice.

**Nothing in a badged product is banned anywhere.** The badge does not ask
whether a formula clears one country's rules. It asks whether any major
cosmetics market prohibits what is in it.

EU Regulation (EC) 1223/2009 is the base, because its list is the longest in
the world: more than 1,700 prohibited substances, and any substance classified
CMR 1A or 1B is added automatically under Article 15. US federal law bans about
30. But the EU is not the strictest on every point, so where another market
bans something Europe still permits, that wins. Japan runs its preservatives
and colourants on approved lists rather than ban lists, which makes it stricter
in those categories than Europe: chlorphenesin is legal in the EU at 0.3% and
not permitted in Japan, so it fails here. Where a US retailer standard (Clean
at Sephora, Target Clean) is stricter than any of them, that wins too.

This is a claim about the formula, not about market access. Selling a product
in China needs an NMPA filing, in the EU a responsible person and a safety
report, and everywhere a local-language label. The badge says nothing about
any of that.

It is also built for textured hair and for Black skin. Generic clean-beauty
lists were written for face cream; the ingredients research keeps finding in
products sold to Black women are the ones this standard names first.

## What it covers

| Family | What qualifies |
|---|---|
| Hair | Formulated hair products. Wigs, extensions, tools and accessories have no formula and are skipped. |
| Face wash | Rinse-off facial cleansers, including cleansing gels, foams, milks, balms, oils and bars. |
| Body wash | Body washes, shower gels, bar soaps, hand soaps, castile soaps and bubble baths. |
| Skincare | Everything else in skincare that stays on the skin: serums, moisturisers, oils, butters, masks, toners, exfoliants, lip care, deodorant and sunscreen. |
| Makeup | Lipstick, gloss, foundation, concealer, powder, blush, eyeshadow, mascara, liner, brows, primers, setting sprays and nail polish. |
| Candles | Judged by their own rules, below, since candles aren't cosmetics. |

Sets, duos and packs are ineligible: one page list cannot vouch for several
formulas. So are tools with no formula: brushes, sponges, rollers, gua sha,
mirrors, false lashes and press-on nails. Household cleaning and laundry are
out of scope for now.

## 1. An ingredient list is published (the gate)

The brand publishes an INCI ingredient list on its product page, and the
rules are checked against that list; whether it is the complete formula is
the brand's statement, not something we can verify. Products with no list
are never badged. Credo requires this of every brand it stocks; the 2026
study of "Target Clean" textured-hair products found only 41% had enough data
to be scored at all.

## 2. Nothing from the ban list

Any listed ingredient in these groups fails the product. "Formulated without"
means under 100 ppm, matching Target's published definition, so trace
contaminants a brand cannot list do not count.

| Group | Examples | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Formaldehyde and releasers | formaldehyde, methylene glycol, DMDM hydantoin, quaternium-15, imidazolidinyl and diazolidinyl urea, bronopol, sodium hydroxymethylglycinate | Formaldehyde is on EU Annex II. The EU requires a "releases formaldehyde" warning above 10 ppm (Reg. 2022/1181). The FDA's proposed ban in straighteners has missed six deadlines and is still unpublished. |
| Parabens | any *-paraben* | The EU bans five outright on Annex II and caps propyl- and butylparaben at 0.14% on Annex V. A concentration cap cannot be verified from a list, so all parabens fail. |
| Phthalates, nonylphenol ethoxylates | DEHP, DBP, BBP, nonoxynol | EU Annex II. Helm et al. 2018 found diethyl phthalate across Black hair products. |
| Cyclic silicones | D4, D5, D6, cyclotetrasiloxane, cyclopentasiloxane, cyclomethicone | D4 has been on EU Annex II since 2022. REACH caps D5 and D6 at 0.1% in both rinse-off and leave-on from June 2026. Helm 2018 measured cyclosiloxanes at the highest concentration of anything in anti-frizz products. |
| Zinc pyrithione | pyrithione | EU Annex II since March 2022, reprotoxic 1B (Reg. 2021/1902). Still routine in US anti-dandruff shampoo. |
| Lilial and Lyral | butylphenyl methylpropional, hydroxyisohexyl 3-cyclohexene carboxaldehyde | Both banned outright on EU Annex II. They are fragrance allergens the EU decided could not be used safely at any level. |
| Microplastics | polyethylene, polypropylene, PMMA, nylon-12, PTFE as particles | EU REACH Reg. 2023/2055. Exfoliating microbeads in rinse-off products have been banned since October 2023. Polyethylene *glycol* is a water-soluble liquid and does not count. |
| EDTA and its salts | tetrasodium EDTA, disodium EDTA, edetate | Excluded by the EU Ecolabel for cosmetics on biodegradability grounds. Clean at Sephora added it in 2024. |
| SLS and SLES | sodium lauryl sulfate, sodium laureth sulfate | Stricter than the EU, which permits both. Excluded by Clean at Sephora and Target Clean; ethoxylated surfactants carry 1,4-dioxane as a contaminant. |
| Ethanolamines | DEA, MEA, TEA, cocamide DEA | EU Annex II bans diethanolamine and caps nitrosamine content. Excluded at Sephora and Target. |
| Antimicrobials and preservatives | triclosan, triclocarban, methylisothiazolinone, methylchloroisothiazolinone | The FDA removed triclosan and triclocarban from soap in 2016. The EU bans MIT in leave-on and caps rinse-off at 15 ppm, which a list cannot prove. |
| Heavy metals and tar | lead acetate, mercury, thimerosal, coal tar | EU Annex II. The FDA withdrew its lead acetate hair-dye approval in 2018. Mercury adulteration is a documented problem in skin-lightening products. |
| The rest of the retailer consensus | hydroquinone, PFAS, BHA, BHT, toluene, resorcinol, p-phenylenediamine, synthetic and polycyclic musks | On EU Annex II or Annex III, or on both the Clean at Sephora and Target Clean exclusion lists. Musks are also excluded by the EU Ecolabel. |
| Sunscreen filters | 4-MBC, oxybenzone and other benzophenones, octinoxate, octocrylene | Palau banned "reef-toxic" sunscreens in 2020, the first country to, naming these filters. The EU banned 4-MBC outright from May 2025 (Reg. 2024/996) as an endocrine disruptor. Clean at Sephora excludes the rest. |
| Palau's sunscreen rule | phenoxyethanol, in a sunscreen only | Palau's 2020 law also bans phenoxyethanol in sunscreens. It is a sunscreen rule, so phenoxyethanol fails only in products with SPF; everywhere else it is a permitted preservative. |
| Retinyl palmitate | retinyl palmitate | Excluded by both Clean at Sephora and Target Clean. The EU caps vitamin A at 0.05% in body lotion and 0.3% elsewhere (Reg. 2024/996). Retinol itself is allowed: capped in the EU, banned nowhere, and excluded by no retailer standard. |
| Banned outside Europe | chlorphenesin, iodopropynyl butylcarbamate, Rhodamine B | Europe permits all three; Japan does not. Its preservatives and colourants run on approved lists, so anything left off cannot be used there at any concentration. Chlorphenesin is legal in the EU at 0.3% and IPBC at 0.01–0.02%. |

## 3. Fragrance is disclosed to the EU standard

"Fragrance", "parfum", "aroma" or "scent" alone fails. The product passes only
when the list also names the fragrance allergens it contains, or carries no
fragrance at all. Named essential oils are ingredients, not fragrance, and
pass.

The allergen list is the one in EU Regulation (EU) 2023/1545, which expanded
the set that must be individually declared from 24 to more than 80 and takes
effect for new products on 31 July 2026. A handful of those names double as
something else (menthol and camphor are actives, benzyl benzoate a solvent), so
they count as allergens but cannot on their own prove a disclosure. Two
unambiguous names are required.

This is the rule that separates the badge from a US retailer's "clean" shelf:
70% of Target Clean textured-hair products listed undisclosed fragrance, and it
drove most of their moderate-hazard scores.

## 4. Candles

Candles aren't cosmetics, so the lists above don't govern them, and almost
nothing in a candle is banned anywhere. The badge keeps its meaning by asking
the same three questions of what a candle is made of (catalog/candle_badge.py):

- **Materials are published.** The listing names its wax and its wick.
- **Nothing banned anywhere.** Lead-core wicks are banned in the US (CPSC,
  2003) and Australia (2002); any metal-core wick fails.
- **Fragrance is disclosed.** A candle sold in the EU must name its fragrance
  allergens on the label (CLP, EUH208), but listings almost never do. The badge
  asks for the nearest thing a listing can show: essential oils, a fragrance
  stated phthalate-free, or no scent at all. "Fragrance oil" alone fails.

Paraffin wax is allowed and noted: it isn't banned anywhere. Candle sets fail
for the same reason cosmetic sets do.

## 5. Excluded categories, whatever the formula

- **Relaxers, texturizers, keratin, Brazilian and smoothing treatments.**
  Relaxer use has an established association with uterine and breast cancer
  risk, and straightener formaldehyde exposure is the subject of the FDA's
  proposed rule.
- **Permanent hair colour.** It runs on p-phenylenediamine and resorcinol.
- **Skin bleaching and lightening.** This is the product class that mercury and
  hydroquinone adulteration keeps turning up in, and it is marketed hard to
  Black shoppers. "Brightening" is not the same claim and is not excluded.

## Allowed, and noted in the report

- **Petrolatum and mineral oil.** Sephora bans them, Target does not. Hair
  grease is a real tradition in Black hair care and the health evidence is weak.
- **Linear silicones** (dimethicone, amodimethicone). A hair-preference
  question, not a safety one. A "silicone-free" filter belongs in search.
- **Carbon black (CI 77266)** in mascara and liner. It is a permitted colourant
  in the EU and US, and not coal tar.
- **Retinol**, salicylic acid, alpha-arbutin and kojic acid: capped in some
  markets, banned in none, excluded by no retailer standard.
- **Talc.** Clean at Sephora restricts it over asbestos contamination; no market
  bans it.
- **Saponified oils in traditional soap.** Cold-process soap lists the lye it
  was made from. Saponification consumes it, none is left in the bar, and the
  EU's own INCI convention names the saponified oils. African black soap and
  shea bars are not penalised for it.

## Markets covered, and the gap

Verified against the EU (Regulation 1223/2009, Annexes II, III and V), the US
(FDA prohibitions and the Clean at Sephora and Target Clean retailer lists),
the EU Ecolabel, and the specific Japanese prohibitions listed above.

Not yet read in full: the Chinese, South Korean, Canadian and Japanese national
lists as complete documents. China prohibits about 1,388 substances and South
Korea about 1,000, but both were built from the EU's Annex II and are largely
contained in it; Canada's Hotlist runs to roughly 500 entries. The remainder is
mostly substances that never appear in consumer cosmetics. These lists are
published as database records rather than downloadable tables, so closing the
gap properly means licensing a regulatory database (Global CosIng, Formpli)
rather than scraping. Until that happens, the honest reading of this standard
is: nothing the EU bans, nothing the EU Ecolabel excludes, nothing the named US
retailer standards exclude, and the Japanese prohibitions recorded above.

## What the badge does not check

Concentration limits, contaminant levels, whether a product is registered to
sell in a given country, and the EU's animal-testing ban (Article 18) cannot be
read off an ingredient list. Where the EU sets a cap
rather than a ban, this standard bans the ingredient instead, which is stricter
but blunter. 1,4-dioxane is a contaminant of ethoxylated surfactants rather
than a listed ingredient; banning SLS and SLES is the closest proxy a list
allows. A brand attestation would be needed to do better.

## Mechanics

- Lists are read from the product page once and stored in
  `catalog/ingredients.json` so the badge is reproducible from the repo. Pages
  with no list are retried after 30 days.
- `clean: true` is written onto passing products in the catalog and is absent
  otherwise. The app shows `CleanBadge` wherever it shows the organic badge, and
  the product page explains the standard on tap.
- `catalog/clean_report.md` lists every failing product with a list and why.
  Read it after each build; a new false positive in the extractor shows up
  there first.
- Household cleaning and laundry have draft criteria but are not implemented:
  quaternary ammonium disinfectants and chlorine bleach banned on the asthma
  evidence, a 1,4-dioxane attestation under 1 ppm, EPA Safer Choice or EWG
  Verified auto-qualifying, and disinfectants never badged.
